# Money Quest Adventure Journals: three age-specific paid editions
Status: DESIGN SPECIFICATION ONLY. No finished paid PDFs, illustrations, translations or archives are claimed.
Date: 2026-11-02. Owner request: separate 6–8, 9–11, 12–14 journals with financial literacy, entrepreneurship and leadership sections.

## Product architecture
Create THREE separately designed journals, each divided into exactly THREE major parts:
1. Money and financial literacy
2. Entrepreneurship
3. Leadership
Each journal begins with a child-name line, 'How to use this journal' pictorial page, a **fully worked example**, and a visual legend for trace / circle / match / cut / glue / draw / write / choose. Include progress badges and a completion certificate. Do not put the child's name online.
Retain clear A4 print layout, 25mm left binding margin, readable contrast, color and reduced-distraction editions, and a separate 7-page adult guide per age band. Activities must be completable with pencil, pointing, AAC, typing or adult support as appropriate. No requirement to write full sentences for Explorer.
Every activity: title, one explicit action, a worked example where needed, the actual activity area, optional extension, and website topic reference. Keep printouts functional in black and white.

## Explorer 6–8: play and discover
Financial literacy: (1) cut and sort coins / notes / card / phone / stones / leaves into 'money or a way to pay' vs 'not money'; distinguish card/phone as PAYMENT TOOLS, not money themselves. (2) Trace dotted paths matching coins to values using UK £ denominations. (3) Cut/glue a picture shopping basket under a budget. (4) Picture maze to reach a savings goal. (5) Match needs vs wants. (6) Four-piece coin picture puzzle. (7) Find-and-circle payment tools in a scene. (8) Count and color coins. (9) Sequencing: earn/save/spend/share. (10) Choice board: what would you do?
Entrepreneurship: (1) choose a stall idea using picture cards; (2) cut and glue items onto a stand; (3) design a shop sign; (4) match customer request to product; (5) picture-sort 'costs money to make' vs 'ready to sell'; (6) simple price tags with whole pounds; (7) role-play a friendly customer greeting; (8) choose an improvement after a customer comment.
Leadership: (1) identify helpful vs unhelpful actions in pictures; (2) complete a turn-taking sequence; (3) match emotions to helpful responses; (4) choose how to fix an accidental block-tower fall; (5) cut and assemble a team puzzle; (6) draw or select a kind invitation; (7) choose a fair sharing plan; (8) team celebration badge.
Design: large illustrated scenes, one action per page, high visual-to-text ratio, no decorative clutter around answer zones. Scissors/glue with adult supervision; alternative sticker/pointing version.

## Builder 9–11: apply and explain
Financial literacy: identify physical vs digital money and payment methods; calculate change; compare value vs price; needs/wants budget; weekly savings tracker; supermarket unit-value exercise; spot advertising urgency; choose a safe online purchase.
Entrepreneurship: product idea, customer profile, cost/price/profit with small whole-number sums, lemonade-stand A/B sign test, packaging sketch, customer feedback, basic ethical advertising, 3-step mini launch.
Leadership: cooperative planning, team roles, listening and disagreement comic strip, repair a mistake, fairness dilemma, group budget decision, feedback and self-reflection.
Use illustrated scenarios and gradually reduced scaffolding; mix matching, short writing, diagrams and calculations.

## Strategist 12–14: reason and evaluate
Financial literacy: payment security, spending trade-offs, saving targets, compound-growth illustration clearly labelled as hypothetical, cost comparison, advertising manipulation, subscriptions and consumer choices.
Entrepreneurship: value proposition, research interview, unit costs, margin vs profit, break-even with worked example, customer feedback iteration, ethical marketing, risk and budget.
Leadership: competing stakeholder needs, constructive feedback, decision matrix, conflict resolution, delegation, inclusion, persuasive but fair communication, project retrospective.
Use realistic but fictional scenarios, multi-step analysis, spreadsheets-on-paper, reflection and optional group tasks. No financial product recommendation or investment promises.

## Example of a truly clear Explorer activity
Page title: 'What can we use to pay?'
Picture instruction: 'Cut out the pictures. Glue them in the right box.'
Worked example: a £1 coin is already pictured inside 'Money'; a debit card is already pictured inside 'Way to pay'.
Three destination boxes: 'Money' (coins and notes), 'Ways to pay' (debit card and phone with payment app), 'Not money' (leaf, pebble, toy).
Pictures to cut: £2 coin, £5 note, debit card, phone, leaf, stone, toy car.
Optional accessible alternative: 'Point to each picture. Ask someone to place it for you.'
Important accuracy: cards and phones are tools for accessing or transferring money, not themselves currency.

## Illustrations and covers

Consistent Money Quest characters, large subject-specific illustrations on EVERY activity, printable cut-out sheets with clear dotted lines and safe margins, differentiated icons for the three parts. No generated image may contradict the learning objective. Check every picture against the written answer key before release.
Covers must visibly say 'Printable digital journal. Not a physical printed book.' Adult guide explains preparation, accessibility and suggested use.
A child should be able to understand the FIRST activity after viewing the one worked example. Pilot with an adult unfamiliar with the journal and ideally children in each age band before sale.

## Research basis
OECD/European Commission Financial Competence Framework for Children and Youth (2023): competencies develop by age; published groupings 6–10, 11–15, 16–18, not identical to our product bands.
EEF Early Years Toolkit, Play-based learning: guided play promising, evidence limited.
EEF Metacognition and Self-regulation (2025): explicit modelling, planning, monitoring and reflection.
These sources inform design principles, not proof that this specific journal improves outcomes.
