> Help children distinguish evidence from confidence, advertising and persuasive language.

## Start with claims children encounter

Use a fictional poster claiming 'Everyone needs this toy'. Ask who made the claim, what evidence is offered and what the poster wants the reader to do. Children can recognise persuasive language before learning formal logic.

## Model how to change your mind

Say aloud: 'I thought this was cheaper, but I forgot delivery. I will compare the total price.' This shows that revising a decision is part of good thinking, not failure.

## Use a three-question routine

Ask 'What is the claim?', 'How could we check?', and 'What else might explain it?' Use familiar contexts such as prices, online recommendations or game rewards. Avoid turning every conversation into a debate.

## Teach uncertainty

Some questions have clear answers, such as whether £4 plus £3 fits a £6 budget. Others depend on priorities. Encourage children to say 'I am not sure yet' when information is missing.

## Measure reasoning, not agreement

Present a new scenario and ask the child to identify missing evidence or propose a fair comparison. A child who respectfully disagrees and explains why may be showing stronger thinking than one who repeats the adult's choice.

## Try this next

Choose one activity from this guide and repeat it with a different fictional example. Ask the child to explain a choice, show it with pictures or communicate it using their preferred method. Keep real family financial information private.
