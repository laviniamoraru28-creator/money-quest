> A practical age-by-age approach to talking about earning, spending, saving and giving, without turning every family decision into a money lesson.

## Start with the money decisions children can see

Children learn more when money has a purpose they recognise. At the shop, compare two similar products and explain why you choose one. At home, talk about the difference between paying for something now and saving for later. You do not need to reveal household income or debt. Use simple, fictional examples if discussing real finances feels uncomfortable.

## Ages 6 to 8: recognise, sort and choose

Show coins, notes and safe illustrations of card payments. Ask the child to sort pictures into things we need and things we might want. Give a fictional budget of £5 and let them choose between three items. For a child who does not read yet, use pictures and spoken instructions, with one decision per screen.

## Ages 9 to 11: plan and compare

Set a fictional goal costing £12. If the child saves £3 each week, ask how many weeks it takes. Then introduce a change: what if they spend £3 in week two? Invite them to explain the trade-off, rather than only calculate the answer. Make clear that real families have different budgets.

## Ages 12 to 14: investigate systems and risks

Discuss payslips in simplified form, digital payments, advertising, subscriptions and online scams. Ask the child to explain how they would verify an unexpected payment request. Use hypothetical accounts and avoid sharing real banking details.

## A ten-minute family routine

Choose one decision, model your thinking aloud, let the child choose, and ask what they would change next time. Repeat weekly with new examples. Progress means explaining a reason for a decision, not simply remembering a definition.

## Try this next

Choose one activity from this guide and repeat it with a different fictional example. Ask the child to explain a choice, show it with pictures or communicate it using their preferred method. Keep real family financial information private.
