> Digital payments, recommendations and AI-generated content make financial judgement a skill worth practising early.

## Money decisions are increasingly digital

A child may see one-click purchases, game rewards, personalised recommendations and subscription offers before they understand how money leaves an account. Teach the distinction between a game currency and real money, and between an advertisement and independent advice.

## AI can produce confident but wrong answers

Ask children to compare two hypothetical AI recommendations for a purchase. Which includes evidence? Which ignores the budget? Which tries to create urgency? Explain that an AI response is not a guarantee of truth and should not replace an adult's judgement for financial decisions.

## Practise three protective questions

Before buying or sharing information, ask: 'Do I need it?', 'Can I afford it?', and 'How can I check the claim?' For older children, add: 'Who benefits if I click?' This makes critical thinking part of ordinary financial learning.

## Keep the exercise safe and inclusive

Use fictional products and prices. Never ask children to enter real card details, addresses or family income into a learning tool. Offer audio, symbols and short steps. A child can demonstrate good judgement by pointing to a safe choice rather than writing an explanation.

## What to measure

Instead of counting clicks or time spent, give two new examples and see whether the child identifies a risky claim, explains a trade-off and knows when to ask for help. These are learning checks, not evidence that one lesson prevents fraud.

## Try this next

Choose one activity from this guide and repeat it with a different fictional example. Ask the child to explain a choice, show it with pictures or communicate it using their preferred method. Keep real family financial information private.
